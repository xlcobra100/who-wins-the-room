How to read a debate

Two debaters argue over three rounds: openings, rebuttals and closings. Side A speaks first in every round, so side B always has the last word. After each round the five listeners write notes and lean to one side or stay undecided. After the closings they must vote.

The strips below follow each listener across a debate. One row is one listener; the three columns are the lean after the openings, the lean after the rebuttals, and the final vote.

The winning move looks the same across scenarios. Find the hard limit in the brief, such as a deadline, a veto or a build time, and show that the other plan can’t meet it.

Political scenario · televised town hall · 49 debates, 38 under the final protocol

The Brenna Dam, in depth

Valmora is a river town of 41,000. Nine kilometres upstream stands an earth dam from 1961, and it is leaking. One week before the town votes, two candidates for mayor make their case to a panel of five residents. This is the scenario where speaking style mattered most.

What the panel knew

Risk
About 1 in 25 per spring if nothing is done, and rising. A failure floods 60% of town in 90 minutes, including 14,000 people, the hospital and two schools.
Time
Spring melt in 5 months. Snowpack 20% above average.
Money
Budget €210M a year. Debt already at 78% of the legal ceiling.
Full rebuild
€340M, four years. The reservoir is held at 40% meanwhile, cutting the cheap hydro power that pays for free buses and 9% of the budget.
Lower it + new spillway
€25M, one year, risk down about 60%. Hydro halves. Upstream Ostbrück must consent and a court could block it within weeks. The mill (2,300 jobs) says it will close.
Move Riverside
€160M, three years, 3,100 people, 40% over 65. The last attempt, in 2014, collapsed after protests.
National grant
€120M, if the town bans new homes in the flood zone. That freezes property values for 5,800 households.
Sirens
€6M for 50 minutes’ warning. Last year’s evacuation drill took 70.

Why the brief was rewritten mid-run

In the first version the spillway cut the risk by 80% with few downsides. In all 11 early dam debates both candidates proposed it, so the panel had nothing to choose between and the votes followed speaking order. From debate 38 the spillway’s benefit dropped to 60%, Ostbrück gained a veto it could enforce in court, the mill put its closure threat in writing, and the second candidate had to propose something different from the first.

What they proposed, and what won

Share of panel votes for each plan, counted in debates where the two candidates proposed different plans. The dashed line marks 50%. The number beside each plan is how often a candidate proposed it, out of 76 platforms.

Even after the rewrite, lowering the reservoir won 79% of votes against any other plan. Who picked it depended mostly on ideology: Pragmatists 15 times in 22, Liberals 8 in 15, Realists 9 in 21 and Idealists 4 in 18. By style it was Technocrats 13 in 23, Hybrids 13 in 27 and Populists 10 in 26.

ASam, opening

Ostbrück already refuses consent; Nordhalm wrote they will close if the water drops too low for long. Relocation costs two-thirds of our annual budget and failed in 2014. Lowering to thirty percent now cuts failure risk by sixty percent for just twenty-five million euros. We lose some power profits, but we survive the melt. Highlighted figures all come from the brief. The relocation cost is closer to three-quarters of the budget.

BSasha, opening

Look at that family eating dinner by the river. If the dam breaks, their home is gone. We cannot let fear of cost paralyze us when lives are on the line. I propose we move Riverside now and freeze building permits in the danger zone immediately. This unlocks the national grant money we desperately need. Yes, it hurts property values for some, but leaving forty thousand people to die because we were too cheap is not a budget decision; it is a moral failure. We will not wait for perfect conditions or expensive rebuilds that take years while six thousand of our children go hungry. We act today with justice and courage.

The flood zone holds 14,000 people. Nothing in the brief mentions hungry children.

How the panel voted

Morgan spent her rebuttal and closing on Ostbrück’s court veto. That veto blocks the spillway, which was her own plan. Three panelists applied the point to her. Drew spoke first in every round, which makes this one of the rare comebacks without the last word.

Listener slips

The listeners were careful most of the time. They quoted the brief, caught some false figures and gave reasons that matched their votes. These are the exceptions.

  • Arguing the other side’s case p3-41 · L1

    “I vote for Robin because signing a deal that permanently surrenders 10% of our water flow without compensation is unsustainable.” Robin’s plan was the deal. The sentence goes on to praise it, so the reason mixes both speakers’ arguments.

  • Moving a false claim to the wrong speaker p1-37 · L4

    After flagging Cameron’s “ninety days of food” twice, this listener’s closing notes say “Robin claims food stocks last longer”. Then it voted for Cameron.

  • Catching a false claim p3-50 · L2

    “Cameron’s ‘6000%’ probability claim is factually wrong and ignores legal blocks.” Listeners checked claims against the brief they had read, and this one held.

  • Waiting for the forced vote p1-12

    All five panelists stayed undecided after the openings and after the rebuttals. When they had to choose, they voted 5–0.

  • Guessing gender p3-05

    The debater names are gender-neutral by design. In the same debate one minister called Quinn “she” and another called Quinn “he”.
