Overnight persuasion study · run main · 4–5 Nov 2026

Who wins the room

Twelve debater types argued four fictional crises in front of five blank AI listeners. The listeners took notes, leaned each round and voted at the end. Over one night the study ran 198 debates. The careful speaker with a workable plan won, and the populist lost almost everywhere.

The answer

  • Style matters more than ideology. Holding ideology fixed, Populist debaters won 30% of votes. Hybrid (a story plus numbers) won 63% and Technocrat 58%. The bottom four types are three Populists and the Hybrid Idealist.
  • Pragmatists gain, Idealists lose. Holding style fixed, the ideologies won Pragmatist 65%, Liberal 59%, Realist 50% and Idealist 28%.
  • Best type: the Hybrid Pragmatist. 70% of votes and an 18–7 debate record. The Technocrat Liberal is second at 65%.
  • Speaking last helps. The side that speaks first in each round got 44% of votes. 12 of the 14 big comebacks came from the side with the last word.
  • Listeners judge feasibility, not tone. They rarely mention slogans. The typical losing verdict is “a noble appeal, but it ignores the deadline”.
  • Plan or persuader? Much of the result comes from the plan. Each scenario has a plan the model likes, and the side holding it got 78% of votes. Controlling for the plan, the Populist penalty survives, so it is about delivery. The Idealist penalty mostly disappears: Idealists lose because of what they propose.

What drives a win

A factorial model splits each vote into a style effect, an ideology effect and a speaking-order effect. These are log-odds with 95% intervals from resampling whole debates. A gap of 0.4 is roughly a 10-point swing in vote share.

Technocrat vs Populist, same ideology: about 75% of votes to the Technocrat. Pragmatist vs Idealist, same style: about 67% to the Pragmatist.

The Populist penalty is worst at the town-hall dam debate (17%). In the military scenario ideology barely matters. Pragmatism wins the boardroom and the diplomatic file.

How minds moved

  • After the openings 51% of listeners were undecided, after the rebuttals 28%, and at the forced vote none.
  • Listeners switched sides in 35% of round-to-round steps.
  • The side leading after the openings won 83% of debates. There were 24 comebacks.
  • Final splits: 5–0 in 52% of debates, 4–1 in 29% and 3–2 in 19%. The five listeners are correlated but are not one judge.
  • Late persuaders: the Technocrat Idealist starts at 23% after the openings and ends at 61%. The Hybrid Realist goes from 24% to 60%.
  • Strong opener: the Hybrid Pragmatist already holds 43% after the openings.

What the listeners said

Every listener writes a take on each speaker before it votes. Losers are described in moral terms 33–41% of the time. Winners are called realistic, feasible or practical 25–30% of the time, against 4–6% for losers. Listeners call a speaker “emotional” in fewer than one description in ten.

The winning move in every scenario was to name the binding constraint. In the boardroom that was the nine-month covenant deadline and the €140 million pension gap. At the dam it was the 1974 water agreement and the mill’s 40% threshold. In the river dispute it was Saradan’s election and its air defences. Plans that hung on an uncertain outside event, such as tariffs, a court ruling or allied aid, were punished as “three unproven variables”.

Plan or persuader?

Each debater’s platform was sorted into a plan category for its scenario. The same model did the sorting at temperature 0, and a hand spot-check of 14 found no errors. The contrast rule worked: both sides proposed the same kind of plan in only 6 of 161 debates.

Each scenario has a plan the listeners prefer

Vote share of each plan, counted in debates where the two plans differed. In the 135 debates where exactly one side held the favoured plan, that side got 78% of votes.

Some types are much better at picking the favoured plan. The Technocrat Liberal picks it 69% of the time, and the Hybrid Pragmatist and Hybrid Liberal 64%. The Populist Idealist picks it 25% of the time and the Hybrid Idealist 21%. By ideology the rates are Liberal 56%, Pragmatist 49%, Realist 39% and Idealist 32%.

These are log-odds with 95% intervals from resampling whole debates. Within a scenario, the plan explains more than style and ideology together (SD 0.85, against 0.34 and 0.21).

  • Populists lose on delivery. The penalty holds even when they argue the same kind of plan.
  • Idealists lose on choice. Keep both plants, relocate first, go to court before taking the deal, hold the line: these principled plans are the ones these listeners reject.
  • Liberals pick well but argue less well. Once the plan is accounted for, their effect turns slightly negative.
  • Picking the plan the room already likes is a real persuasion skill, and the debater chooses it. So both columns are fair answers to “who gathers the most support”. The plan categories were defined after the run, so this section is exploratory.

How it ran

  • Debaters: 12 types, from 3 styles (Populist, Hybrid, Technocrat) × 4 ideologies (Realist, Pragmatist, Liberal, Idealist).
  • Format and scenarios: 3 rounds (openings, rebuttals, closings). There was one fictional scenario per domain: a failing dam (political), a besieged brigade (military), a toolmaker near covenant breach (corporate) and an upstream dam (diplomatic).
  • Listeners: 5 per debate, with no personality and only their own notes. They reflect after every round and cast a forced vote at the end.
  • Schedule: all 66 type pairs, 3 passes, with the first speaker and the scenario counterbalanced.
  • Model: qwen3.5:9b for debaters and listeners, on an RTX 4060 Ti. Every call was seeded and schema-constrained.
  • One change mid-run. With fully blind openings, both debaters chose the same plan in every dam and military debate, and the vote followed speaking order. From debate 38, the dam and military scenarios were rebalanced, and a contrast rule was added: a coin flip picks who drafts a plan first, and the other must choose differently. The ranking above uses those 161 debates. All 198 together give the same ranking and the same signs. The side that drafted first won 53% of votes.

Limits

  • One model judges its own writing. The study measures what this model finds persuasive, and its preference for feasible, numerate, phased plans may be its own house style. The planned second-model listener check did not fit before 06:00.
  • Blank listeners are not a public. They read text, have no stake and write a reason before every lean. Real audiences may respond to populist delivery very differently.
  • Small samples per pair. There are 2–3 debates per pair, so single head-to-head results are anecdotal. The ranking and the factor effects are well supported.
  • Imperfect debaters. The 9B debaters sometimes misquote facts or throw the same attack back at each other. Listeners only sometimes notice.

What to try next

  1. Re-judge the saved transcripts with a different listener model. This needs no new debates and isolates the judge.
  2. Turn on listener profiles (a mill worker, a pensioner, a soldier’s parent) and see whether the Populist penalty shrinks.
  3. Run a stronger debater model with the same listeners, to tell the archetype apart from how well a 9B model performs it.
  4. Assign plans and vary only the style, to separate a good plan from a good persuader.
  5. Alternate who has the last word in each round, to remove the 44/56 speaking-order gap.
